# kebab — the foundation
### Product, brand, and go-to-market doctrine · v1
*Synthesized from discovery, August 2026. This is a living document owned by the maker. Nothing here is sacred except the parts marked as doctrine.*

---

## 0. The one truth

**People will continue to need places to think.**

Every strategic decision below is downstream of this sentence. It is durable for decades, it asks nothing of the roadmap, and it gets *more* true as the world automates. Kebab is a place to think — and a place where the things you think, notice, and catch are **kept on purpose, with their meaning attached**.

---

## 1. The essence

Kebab is a conversation built to be kept.

It sits in the white space between the two forms humans already use:

- **Notes** are durable but *final*. A note is a monument. You don't riff on a monument.
- **Chat** is alive but *disposable*. Conversation is where thinking actually happens — and it is structurally built to be lost. You don't catalog text messages. All of them just happen.

Kebab takes the form of the second and gives it the permanence of the first. The interface is a messaging thread with no recipient: composer at the bottom, "now" always an empty box, the past filing itself politely upward. Because the form is conversation, a bare link feels unfinished — so you say *why*. The context isn't a feature; it's what the chat form extracts from you at the moment of catching.

**The paradox Kebab resolves:** *you can get rid of your thoughts, and still come back to them.* Discharge and retrieval at once. A note app stores thoughts. A chat releases them. Kebab does both.

---

## 2. The enemy

The enemy is not other people. The enemy is **ambience** — the uninvited, infinite stream that buries what mattered.

- Twitter's noise isn't its content (you like the content — you save it). It's other people's activity arriving ambiently and endlessly. A bookmark inside Twitter keeps you inside Twitter.
- A text thread's noise is your real relationship sedimented under three hundred messages about groceries. The revelatory conversation about a quote drowns in logistics.
- In both cases the failure is identical: **the meaningful thing has no place of its own, so it dies in the ambient stream it happened to occur in.**

The line Kebab draws is not *private vs. social*. It is **chosen vs. ambient**. Everything in Kebab — every entry, every collection, eventually every person — is there on purpose. This principle survives the social launch intact. It is load-bearing.

---

## 3. The dramatic center: the flinch

> "Oh shit, that's good — I should write that down."

The capture moment is an involuntary **taste reflex** — a quality-detection event. Something crosses your radar that's too good to lose, and there's a spike of urgency because thoughts like that have a half-life measured in seconds. The reach for the pocket. **The flinch of catching something.**

This is the moment all marketing dramatizes. Not organizing. Not searching. Not "your second brain." The flinch.

**The habit structure (insurance-shaped):**

| Layer | Behavior | Frequency | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Capture | The flinch — link, thought, name, flavor | Daily, many times | The habit. The thing we market. |
| Retrieval | "I *did* write that down" — search, return | Rare | The jackpot. Felt as relief, like past-you left a gift. Users discover it; we never lead with it. |
| The riff | Threads on your own thoughts; ideas built over weeks | Weekly | The soul. The retention engine. Revealed, never announced. |
| Sharing | Invited people around a specific thing | Later | The amplifier and the distribution loop. Discovered, never led with. |

---

## 4. The two registers

The truest thing about Kebab is what one week of real usage looks like: *"the implications of a post-literate society"* sitting directly above *the nephew's birthday ice cream flavor.* The philosophy and the ice cream, on one skewer.

- The **high register**: quotes to riff on, ideas that build across weeks, things learned, taste caught in the wild.
- The **low register**: the receptionist's name at the allergist, what you ordered at that restaurant, the movie list.

The low register is not lesser. Remembering the coffee-shop employee's name is an *act of attentiveness* — it makes you slightly better at being a person. The mix is the charm. A brand that is only highbrow is insufferable; the ice cream keeps it honest. The Moleskine holds grocery lists and manifestos with no shame, and so does Kebab.

---

## 5. Who it's for

**The ICP is a behavior, not a demographic: people who already text themselves.**

Saved Messages. Self-DMs on Slack. The Notes app graveyard. A thousand open tabs. These people have already *invented* Kebab out of desperation — they self-identify instantly ("that's me"), and the product needs zero explanation: it is the purpose-built version of their hack.

Their known failure mode, in their own words: *"I'd text myself a link and then forget why I saved it. No context. The link survived; the reason died."* Kebab's answer: the unit of saving is never the thing — it's **the thing plus what you thought about it.**

Concentric rings outward from the core:

1. **The self-texters** (core): already doing the behavior badly. Conversion cost ≈ zero explanation.
2. **The noticers**: people paying attention to their lives — they collect quotes, remember names, keep lists, feel the flinch and lose the thought. The Moleskine-carrier type, whether or not they carry one.
3. **The design-literate indie-app audience**: people who care who made their software. They overlap heavily with rings 1–2 and they are the evangelist layer — Kebab's craft is legible to them and they narrate it for everyone else.

**Identity rule (the Moleskine move): never say the identity, sell the behavior that implies it.** Moleskine never said "for intellectuals" — it sold carrying a notebook, and Hemingway was implied. The word "intellectual" (or "thinker," "creative," "curator") never appears in Kebab's marketing. "I write things down" is inclusive, humble, and quietly flattering. The user should *feel* like the kind of person who carries a notebook. We never tell them they are.

## 6. Keeping the layers straight

A working map so features, use cases, jobs, benefits, and identity never get mistaken for one another:

| Layer | What it is | Kebab examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Features** | What the software does | Feed, composer, threads, collections-as-chips, resurface, fire, pin, search, link cards, dictation, offline outbox |
| **Use cases** | What a person does with it | Save a tweet away from Twitter; keep restaurant orders; capture a 3am thought; build an idea across weeks; prep topics for a call |
| **Jobs-to-be-done** | Why they hired it | "Catch this before it vanishes." "Keep the reason attached to the thing." "Give my thinking a place of its own." "Let me drop a thought without losing it." |
| **Benefits** | What they get | Relief at capture; the retrieval jackpot; a growing archive of their own mind; escape from ambient noise |
| **Identity** | What using it says | I pay attention to my life. I write things down. |
| **Positioning** | The place it occupies against alternatives | The purpose-built version of texting yourself; conversation built to be kept |

Marketing speaks jobs and identity. The website shows use cases. The App Store lists features last.

## 7. Competitive framing

Kebab does not join a category; it names the gap between two.

| Alternative | What it gets right | Where it fails the user |
|---|---|---|
| **Texting yourself / Saved Messages** | Zero friction, conversational | No structure, no retrieval, context dies, buried under real texts |
| **Apple Notes** | Durable, ubiquitous | Final-feeling; monuments, not conversations; you don't riff on a note; the graveyard effect |
| **Bookmarks / read-it-later (Pocket †2025)** | Easy save | Write-only device. Category king *died* of the graveyard problem. The link survives; the reason dies |
| **mymind and premium savers** | Beautiful, private | Precious; a gallery, not a conversation; storage identity, not thinking identity |
| **The group chat** | Alive, social | Ambient sediment; meaningful exchanges drowned in logistics; nothing findable, nothing kept |

One sentence against all of them: **everything else either keeps things without their meaning, or makes meaning and loses it.**

Category answer, when forced (press, App Store category menus): Kebab is a **notebook that works like a chat**. In public copy we prefer the behavior to the category: *"like texting yourself, but built for it."* We do not coin a category name ("conversational memory," "micro-journaling") — category creation is a venture-scale war, and the humble path is to be *the* purpose-built tool for a behavior everyone already recognizes.

## 8. The funnel: how the wedge opens into the soul

**The link is the bait. The context is the hook. The riff is the habit. The share is the loop.**

1. **Entry (week 1):** "One private feed for the stuff you'd otherwise lose." Concrete, explainable in one breath, matches the trigger a stranger actually has this week (a link in hand, a thought escaping).
2. **Conversion (weeks 1–4):** the chat form makes bare links feel unfinished → they say why → they've started talking to themselves. The composer converts them.
3. **Retention (months):** the feed becomes a place they think. First retrieval jackpot lands ("I *did* write that down") and faith is justified. Threads appear. The archive compounds — **the product appreciates like wine**: year-two retention should beat year one, because every month fed makes the skewer more valuable.
4. **Spread (ongoing):** shared skewers. Every shared collection is a recruitment event where the product demos itself to **exactly the right next user** — someone invited, by someone they trust, into a thing they already care about.

**Positioning principle: depth is revealed, not announced.** The entry story is honest, just incomplete on purpose. We market the flinch; users discover the cathedral.

---

## 9. Progressive positioning: the arc

The short-term story does not evolve into a bigger *claim*. It evolves into a longer *relationship*.

- **Year 1 (the story we tell):** *Kebab is like texting yourself, but built for it. A place for thoughts you don't want to lose.*
- **Year 2–3 (what users say):** "It's where I think. It's where my stuff goes. Send it to my kebab."
- **Year 5 (what it has become, unannounced):** the place a person's inner life accumulated — five years of ice cream flavors and 2am philosophy on one skewer. Shared skewers holding the preserved riffs of friendships, couples, small teams. Not a bigger market position; a deeper one.

The endpoint is not a platform. **Doctrine: hold the doctrine, not the roadmap.** The future (watch capture, desktop, whatever users teach us) is discovered from real usage, and every candidate future passes one gate: *does it add noise?*

**On AI, specifically:** an AI that mines, summarizes, or generates inside Kebab is on the wrong side of the enemy — machine content arriving uninvited into the quiet place is ambience. If AI ever enters, it is **retrieval, not generation**: helping you find your own thought, never speaking over it. It hands you your own words back; it never adds words of its own.

## 10. Product doctrine

The rules that outrank features, roadmaps, and revenue:

1. **Chosen, not ambient.** Everything in Kebab is there on purpose. Nothing arrives uninvited — no feeds of others, no machine-generated content, no notifications that aren't yours.
2. **The archive is never hostage.** A user can always read, search, and export everything they ever put in — lapsed, free, or furious. Payment gates the *pen*, never the *notebook*.
3. **Depth is revealed, not announced.** Market the flinch. Let the riff, the retrieval, and the sharing be discovered. This applies to copy, onboarding, and the name of every feature.
4. **Grow features, never noise.** The gate for every addition: does it protect attention or spend it?
5. **The invited side is always free.** No paywall ever touches the recipient of a shared thought. Joining, riffing on someone else's skewer: free, with a small allowance of their own entries. Paying is for having your own skewer.
6. **The object is the hero.** The maker posts, but the product fronts. Craft is the marketing.
7. **Never say the identity.** Sell the behavior; let the identity be implied. The words "intellectual," "thinker," "second brain," and "productivity" are banned from all copy.
8. **The recommendation-sentence test.** Every name — feature, tier, verb — must survive being said at a bar: *"Dude, get kebab — it's like texting yourself, but it's actually built for it."* If a real human wouldn't say it, it doesn't ship.

## 11. What kebab means (beyond features)

**Emotional territory owned: attention.** Kebab belongs to people who are paying attention to their lives. If you are tapped in — watching the world, noticing it, engaging with it — thoughts worth keeping arrive constantly. Kebab is the companion to an attentive life. The more engaged with the world you are, the more you use it — and the causality runs both ways: carrying a place to catch things makes you notice more. *(The bike-for-the-mind lineage, one turn further: a place that makes the thinking itself compound.)*

**The cultural position:** a love letter to technology — the kind that lets you think, against the kind that thinks at you. Kebab is not anti-tech nostalgia; it is pro-technology, on the side of your attention. In an ever-automated world, staying a person who thinks is the differentiating act. Kebab is quietly on the side of the humans.

**What using kebab says (never stated, always implied):** *I notice things. I write things down. My own mind is worth keeping.*

## 12. Personality, voice, and vibe

**The character:** street food holding philosophy. Humble on the outside, deep on the inside, funny about itself, dead serious about its craft. Good at 2am.

**Personality axes:**
- Warm, not precious. (mymind is a gallery; kebab is a pocket.)
- Wry, not jokey. One good laugh line beats three puns.
- Calm, not sleepy. The energy of a quiet room, not a spa.
- Precise, not technical. The craft shows in the details, never in the jargon.
- Confident, not loud. It doesn't care about being cool, which is cool.

**Voice rules:**
- Lowercase wordmark always: **kebab**. The name refuses to take itself seriously so the contents can.
- Short sentences at the moments that matter. The copy should feel like an entry.
- Speak to the behavior, never the identity (doctrine #7).
- Self-aware about the name once, gladly: *"Why kebab? Because it's a bunch of unrelated things threaded together. And it's good at 2am."* This answer is a distribution asset — people enjoy retelling it.
- Never: "supercharge," "capture ideas effortlessly," "your thoughts, organized," "second brain," "productivity," "unleash."

**In-app copy is brand copy.** The empty state, the paywall, the changelog — same voice everywhere. The changelog has an author's voice (updates as small events, ConcernedApe-style), even while the maker stays behind the object.

## 13. The visual world

**What already exists (and is right):**
- Near-black background `#171718`, soft off-white text `#CAD0DB` — a dark, quiet room.
- **Sticky-note yellow `#EBCF47`** — the single warm artifact in the dark. This is the brand's signature move: one humble, analog, warm object against calm darkness. Protect it.
- Purple send `#935ED5`, amber resurface `#FFC57C`, pink fire `#F79CE1` — small jewels of color that only appear when *you* act. Color = intention. This is "chosen vs. ambient" expressed as a palette.
- DM Sans (body) + JetBrains Mono (wordmark) — human voice, craftsman's label.

**Direction for everything outside the app:**
- **The dark room and the yellow note** is the poster. Marketing surfaces are calm and dark; the sticky note (or the caught thought) is the light source.
- **The skewer as motif:** disparate small things threaded on one line. As illustration, as layout system (a vertical feed line threading unlike items: a quote, a photo, an ice cream cone, a link card), as animation.
- **Photography, if used:** the world being noticed, not people using phones. The 2am food cart. The city walk. A receipt with something scribbled on it. The moment before the flinch. Never stock, never desks, never someone smiling at a device.
- **The two registers side by side** in every composition: the profound and the practical on one skewer. This juxtaposition *is* the visual identity: "post-literate society" above "mint chip — nephew's bday."
- Marketing typography follows the app: DM Sans for speech, mono for the small true labels.

## 15. The deal (pricing)

- **Hero price: $20/year.** *A Moleskine costs about twenty dollars. So does a year of kebab.* Modest, physical, notebook-priced — a comparison no competitor has the nerve to make. 50,000 people = $1M/yr, and they compound instead of needing replacement.
- **Monthly:** $2.49–2.99, priced so annual is obviously sane.
- **Founding tier at launch:** first cohort (e.g., first 1,000–10,000) gets lifetime for ~$50 or founding-year pricing. "Founding member of kebab" is an identity the tribe will wear; early believers become permanent evangelists.
- **Trial:** one free month — long enough for the flinch to become a habit. When it ends, *the pen goes quiet; the notebook stays open.* Read, search, export forever. No countdown clocks, no deletion threats, ever (doctrine #2).
- **Guests:** invited users write on shared skewers free, always, plus a small allowance of their own entries (enough to feel the flinch, not enough to live in). They convert themselves.
- **The model as brand statement:** no ads, no investors, no data sold. The users fund the place. For a product that is literally a private mind, "you are the customer, not the product" is the security model, said plainly.
- **Honest arithmetic, said out loud when asked:** your thoughts live on servers; recurring cost, recurring revenue. That's the whole business.

## 16. Go-to-market: the quiet craftsman playbook

**The shape:** no blitz. The object is the hero; the maker posts but doesn't perform. Craft is the marketing; the tribe narrates it outward. Every phase feeds the recommendation sentence.

**Phase 0 — Field testing (now, pre-launch):**
- Hand the phone to 3–5 people. Script (see appendix): answer "what is this?" with the recommendation sentence, then *"What's the last thing you texted yourself so you wouldn't lose it? Put that in."* Then silence. What they type is ICP data; whether the feed makes them say "ohh" is product data.
- Watch for: do they add a *why* to a link unprompted? Do they scroll their own two entries with visible satisfaction? Do they ask "can I have this?"

**Phase 1 — Founding cohort (soft launch):**
- TestFlight → paid founding tier. Invite through the tribe's watering holes, personally, in small numbers. The pitch is the name story + the recommendation sentence, nothing more.
- Where the tribe already is: design Twitter/X, indie-app circles (the Things/Bear/Flighty audience), tools-for-thought refugees tired of Notion-weight systems, group chats of founders and noticers. Individual invitations beat announcements.
- Build the changelog voice from day one — updates as small events with an author. This is the ConcernedApe engine at app scale.

**Phase 2 — Public launch:**
- **App Store editorial is the single highest-leverage channel for this species of app.** The pitch to Apple: native SwiftUI craft, Liquid Glass done properly, an indie maker, a humane privacy-respecting model, a design system Apple wants to showcase. Prepare the pitch page and screenshots as carefully as the app.
- **The launch film:** the crescendo scene. Someone walking the city lost in a beautiful, deepening thought — strings building, ideas compounding — abruptly interrupted by *"wait, what did my nephew want for his birthday?"* Record-scratch. Both go into kebab. Both registers, one skewer, thirty seconds, funny and true. This is the brand thesis as an ad.
- Product Hunt / Hacker News: fine as amplifiers on launch day, framed by the name story and the maker's short honest post. Not the center of gravity.
- Press angle, if pursued: not "new notes app" — *"the app for people who text themselves"* + the Pocket-is-dead graveyard angle (everything saves, nothing returns; here's the app built around coming back).

**Phase 3 — The loop (steady state):**
- **Shared skewers are the growth engine:** every invitation is a warm demo to exactly the right next user, delivered by someone they trust, attached to something they already care about. Instrument nothing else until this loop is measured.
- Word-of-mouth artifacts: the name story, the Moleskine price line, the changelog voice. Give people sentences they *enjoy* saying.
- Screenshot-worthiness as a feature: entries, and someday shared riffs, should look good enough to post. Every screenshot of a kebab is an ad with zero spend. *(Also the organic answer to "the wedge is capture, but capture is invisible" — retrieval jackpots and beautiful riffs are what people show off.)*
- Cadence: ship visibly, write the changelog in voice, let updates be small events. A year of consistent small events builds more trust than any campaign.

**What we deliberately do not do:** paid acquisition, category-creation campaigns, influencer pushes, growth hacks that spend attention, launching the social layer as the headline. All of it violates either the scale (humble, solo) or the doctrine (chosen, not ambient).

## 17. Standing tests & heuristics

- **The recommendation-sentence test** — every name, feature, and line: would a human say it at a bar?
- **The noise gate** — every feature and every piece of AI: does it protect attention or spend it?
- **The hostage check** — every monetization decision: does the notebook stay open?
- **The Moleskine check** — every piece of copy: are we selling the behavior, or announcing the identity?
- **The two-registers check** — every marketing composition: is the ice cream next to the philosophy? If it's all philosophy, we've become pretentious; all ice cream, we've become a listicle app.

---

## 18. Open questions (honest list)

1. **Field evidence.** Everything above is built from one (ideal) user. The 3–5 person phone-handing test is the cheapest way to find out which parts are true. Highest priority.
2. **Trademark / App Store diligence on "kebab"** — clearance check, plus how the App Store search results actually look. (Diligence, not doubt.)
3. **The sharing feature's verb and shape** — "share a thought," "invite to a skewer," the exact mechanics — should pass through the recommendation-sentence test and the noise gate before it ships. It's the growth engine; it deserves its own design doc.
4. **Visual references** — the photography/graphics/imagery you're drawn to, so the visual world section can become a real art-direction system.
5. **The watch / voice capture** — aligned with the flinch (lowest-friction catch), but sequencing is a product decision, not a brand one.
6. **Whether "note to self" is the right App Store name suffix** — test against search volume for "text yourself" / "saved messages" behavior terms.

## Appendix A — The field-test kit

**Setup:** your phone, your kebab (real entries visible — the mixed registers are the demo), a person, zero pitch.

1. Hand them the phone unlocked to the feed. Say nothing for ten seconds. *(What they scroll to and ask about first is data.)*
2. When they ask "so what is this?" → **"It's like texting yourself, but built for it. One private feed for the stuff you'd otherwise lose — thoughts, links, little things. You can search it, and you can build on stuff later."**
3. When they ask "what am I supposed to do?" → **"What's the last thing you texted yourself or dumped in Notes so you wouldn't lose it? Put that in."** Then shut up.
4. Watch for: Do they add context to a link unprompted? Do they get the feed metaphor instantly (chat literacy is universal — they should)? Do they ask about privacy? Do they ask "can I get this?" *(the only question that counts)*.
5. Afterward, one question only: **"Who do you know who'd want this?"** Their answer is your ICP, described by the market instead of by us.

**Log per person:** what they typed first · the moment their face changed · their exact words for what it is · who they named.
